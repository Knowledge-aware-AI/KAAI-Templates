# Conducting a Thesis in Our Group

Theses in our group are typically primarily advised by a doctoral researcher, with periodic involvement of Prof. Razniewski. University theses have a somewhat unusual setup: for most of the process, we are your advisors, working with you to submit a strong thesis. At the end, we change hats and become examiners, tasked with evaluating your work fairly according to the standards of the degree.

The following guidance explains how to make us happy as examiners—and, more importantly, how to give your thesis the best chance of succeeding.

## During the thesis

### 1. Report your progress regularly

Please report your current state at least every two weeks. The precise format can differ by case—for example, an in-person meeting, online meeting, or email—but extended periods without contact are a major risk. They remove the opportunity for timely feedback and course corrections.

### 2. Do research, not linear task execution

Some students expect us to “greenlight” a thesis plan that they can then execute linearly. This is not how research works. A rough plan is useful, but the challenging part of research is precisely that later findings may motivate substantial changes to earlier decisions: revising hypotheses and assumptions, redesigning experiments, or changing the analysis.

Proper research is not a single linear *design → implement → analyze* pass.

### 3. Write early

Do not write your thesis in the final two weeks. Start writing on day one. This reduces last-minute stress for you and gives us written material on which we can provide timely, asynchronous feedback.

### 4. Make examples central

A common mistake is to focus only on aggregate metrics such as precision, recall, or accuracy. You need to inspect the actual data to understand whether something works and where it fails.

As in programming, a good research habit is to select a set of “unit-test” cases and observe them systematically throughout the work. The same applies to the written thesis and the defense: a running example is essential for explaining theoretical concepts.

## Thesis defenses

Defenses consist of a 20-minute presentation followed by up to 20 minutes of questions and answers. In the age of generative AI, we take the Q&A part particularly seriously. Expect us to open the thesis at random pages and ask you to explain concepts from it.

## Mandatory outputs

In addition to the written thesis and thesis presentation required by the *Prüfungsordnung* (PO), our group requires:

- An intermediate presentation after approximately three months.
- A data and code repository (GitHub or similar).

Do not postpone these until the end. Use the repository throughout the thesis as part of your communication with us.

Scheduling the intermediate presentation is your responsibility. A good practice is to set the date already in our first meeting. Our group’s default seminar slot is Thursday at 11:00 in room S14-745.

## How we grade

Grades can cause confusion because their intended distribution and their common use differ substantially. According to the *Prüfungsordnung* (PO) for the 2025 Master’s programme in Computer Science, 3.0 should be the average grade, while 2.0 is reserved for work that is significantly above average.

We use the following rubric:

| Grade | Thesis | Presentation | Q&A |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Significant insights on the analyzed problem, backed by technically sound experiments or analyses; excellent overall presentation. | Didactically excellent, well-structured, and well-designed presentation. | Mastery of the technical content and a strong ability to think beyond the immediate scope of the thesis. |
| 2 | Solid insights on the analyzed problem. Technical execution with only minor limitations. Thesis is overall well presented. | Well understandable, with only minor issues, for example in legibility, balance, or pace. | The student owns their topic and can give reasonable answers to all technical questions. |
| 3 | … | … | … |
